## 29.9 Orthogonal Groups and the Cartan–Dieudonn´e

$$Theorem$$
In this section we are dealing with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear from $\phi$  over a finite-dimensional vector space $E$ of dimension $n$ over a field of characateristic not equal to $2$. Recall that the orthogonal group $O(\phi$ ) is the group of isometries of $\phi$ ; that is, the group of linear maps $f$ : $E \to E$ such that
$$\phi (f(u), f(v)) = \phi (u, v)for all u, v \in E.$$
The elements of $O(\phi$ ) are also called orthogonal transformations. If $M$ is the matrix of $\phi$  in any basis, then a matrix $A$ represents an orthogonal transformation iff
$$A^{\top}MA = M.$$
Since $\phi$  is nondegenerate, $M$ is invertible, so we see that det(A) $= \pm 1$. The subgroup
$$SO(\phi ) = {f \in O(\phi ) | \det(f) = 1}$$
is called the special orthogonal group (of $\phi$ ), and its members are called rotations (or proper orthogonal transformations). Isometries $f \in O(\phi$ ) such that det(f) $= -1$ are called improper orthogonal transformations, or sometimes reversions.

If $H$ is any nondegenerate hyperplane in $E$, then $D = H^{\perp}$ is a nondegenerate line and we have
$$E = H^{\perp}\oplus H^{\perp}.$$
For any nonzero vector $u \in D = H^{\perp}$ Consider the map $\tau_{u}$  given by
$$\tau_{u} (v) = v -2 \frac{\phi (v, u)}{\phi (u, u)} ufor all v \in E.$$
If we replace $u$ by $\lambda u$ with $\lambda  \neq  0$, we have
$$\tau_{\lambda u} (v) = v -2 \frac{\phi (v, \lambda u)}{\phi (\lambda u, \lambda u)} \lambda u = v -2 \frac{\lambda \phi (v, u)}{\lambda^{2} \phi (u, u)} \lambda u = v -2 \frac{\phi (v, u)}{\phi (u, u)} u,$$
which shows that $\tau_{u}$  depends only on the line $D$, and thus only the hyperplane $H$. Therefore, denote by $\tau_{H}$  the linear map $\tau_{u}$  determined as above by any nonzero vector $u \in H^{\perp}$. Note that if $v \in H$, then
$$\tau_{H} (v) = v,$$
and if $v \in D$, then
$$\tau_{H} (v) = -v.$$
A simple computation shows that
$$\phi (\tau_{H} (u), \tau_{H} (v)) = \phi (u, v)for all u, v \in E,$$
so $\tau_{H}  \in O(\phi$ ), and by picking a basis consisting of $u$ and vectors in $H$, that $det(\tau_{H}$ ) $= -1$. It is also clear that $\tau_{H}^{2}   =$ id.

> **Definition 29.21.** If $H$ is any nondegenerate hyperplane in $E$, for any nonzero vector $u \in H^{\perp}$, the linear map $\tau_{H}$  given by
$$\tau_{H} (v) = v -2 \frac{\phi (v, u)}{\phi (u, u)} ufor all v \in E$$
is an involutive isometry of $E$ called the reflection through (or about) the hyperplane $H$.
Remarks: $1$. It can be shown that if $f \in O(\phi$ ) leaves every vector in some hyperplane $H$ fixed, then either $f =$ id or $f = \tau_{H}$ ; see Taylor $[180]$ (Chapter $11)$. Thus, there is no analog to symplectic transvections in the orthogonal group. $2$. If $K = \mathbb{R}$ and $\phi$  is the usual Euclidean inner product, the matrices corresponding to hyperplane reflections are called Householder matrices. Our goal is to prove that $O(\phi$ ) is generated by the hyperplane reflections. The following proposition is needed.

> **Proposition 29.39.** Let $\phi$  be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on a vector space $E$. For any two nonzero vectors $u, v \in E$, if $\phi (u, u) = \phi (v, v)$ and $v$ - $u$ is nonisotropic, then the hyperplane reflection $\tau_{H}  = \tau_{v-u}$  maps $u$ to $v$, with $H =$ (K(v - $u))^{\perp}$.

*Proof.* Since $v$ - $u$ is not isotropic, $\phi (v$ - $u, v$ - $u) \neq  0$, and we have
$$-$$
$$\tau_{v-u} (u) = u -2 \frac{\phi (u, vu)}{\phi (v -u, v -u)} (v -u)$$
$$-$$
$$= u -2 \frac{\phi (u, v)\phi (u, u)}{\phi (v, v) -2\phi (u, v) + \phi (u, u)} (v -u)$$
$$-$$
$$= u - \frac{2(\phi (u, v)\phi (u, u))}{2(\phi (u, u) -2\phi (u, v))} (v -u)$$
$$= v,$$
which proves the proposition. We can now obtain a cheap version of the Cartan–Dieudonn´e theorem.

> **Theorem 29.40.** (Cartan–Dieudonn´e, weak form) Let $\phi$  be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on a $K$-vector space $E$ of dimension $n$ (char(K) $\neq  2)$. Then, every isometry $f \in O(\phi$ ) with $f \neq$  id is the composition of at most 2n - $1$ hyperplane reflections.

*Proof.* We proceed by induction on $n$. For $n = 0$, this is trivial (since $O(\phi$ ) $= {id})$. Next, assume that $n \ge 1$. Since $\phi$  is nondegenerate, we know that there is some nonisotropic vector $u \in E$. There are three cases. Case $1$. f(u) $= u$. Since $\phi$  is nondegenrate and $u$ is nonisotropic, the hyperplane $H = (Ku)^{\perp}$ is nondegenerate, $E = H^{\perp}\oplus$ Ku, and since f(u) $= u$, we must have f(H) $= H$. The restriction f' of of $f$ to $H$ is an isometry of $H$. By the induction hypothesis, we can write
$$f'= \tau_{k}'  \circ \cdots  \circ \tau_{1}' ,$$
where $\tau_{i}$  is some hyperplane reflection about a hyperplane $L_{i}$ in $H$, with $k \le$ 2n - $3$. We can extend each $\tau_{i}'$   to a reflection $\tau_{i}$  about the hyperplane $L_{i}^{\perp}\oplus$ Ku so that $\tau_{i} (u) = u$, and clearly,
$$f = \tau_{k}  \circ \cdots  \circ \tau_{1} .$$
Case $2$. f(u) $= -u$. If $\tau$  is the hyperplane reflection about the hyperplane $H = (Ku)^{\perp}$, then $g = \tau  \circ f$ is an isometry of $E$ such that g(u) $= u$, and we are back to Case $(1)$. Since $\tau^{2} = 1$ We obtain
$$f = \tau  \circ \tau_{k}  \circ \cdots  \circ \tau_{1}$$

where $\tau$  and the $\tau_{i}$  are hyperplane reflections, with $k \ge$ 2n - $3$, and we get a total of 2n - $2$ hyperplane reflections. Case $3$. f(u) $\neq  u$ and f(u) $\neq  -u$. Note that f(u) - $u$ and f(u) $+ u$ are orthogonal, since
$$\phi (f(u) -u, f(u) + u) = \phi (f(u), f(u)) + \phi (f(u), u) -\phi (u, f(u)) -\phi (u, u)$$
$$= \phi (u, u) -\phi (u, u) = 0.$$
We also have
$$\phi (u, u) = \phi ((f(u) + u -(f(u) -u))/2, (f(u) + u -(f(u) -u))/2)$$
$$= \frac{1}{4} \phi (f(u) + u, f(u) + u) + \frac{1}{4} \phi (f(u) -u, f(u) -u),$$
so f(u) $+ u$ and f(u) - $u$ cannot be both isotropic, since $u$ is not isotropic. If f(u) - $u$ is not isotropic, then the reflection $\tau_{f(u)-u}$  is such that
$$\tau_{f(u)-u} (u) = f(u),$$
and since $\tau_{f(u)-u}^{2}   =$ id, if $g = \tau_{f(u)-u}  \circ f$, then g(u) $= u$, and we are back to case $(1)$. We obtain
$$f = \tau_{f(u)-u}  \circ \tau_{k}  \circ \cdots  \circ \tau_{1}$$
where $\tau_{f(u)-u}$  and the $\tau_{i}$  are hyperplane reflections, with $k \ge$ 2n - $3$, and we get a total of 2n - $2$ hyperplane reflections. If f(u) $+ u$ is not isotropic, then the reflection $\tau_{f(u)+u}$  is such that
$$\tau_{f(u)+u} (u) = -f(u),$$
and since $\tau_{f(u)+u}^{2}   =$ id, if $g = \tau_{f(u)+u}  \circ f$, then g(u) $= -u$, and we are back to case $(2)$. We obtain
$$f = \tau_{f(u)-u}  \circ \tau  \circ \tau_{k}  \circ \cdots  \circ \tau_{1}$$
where $\tau , \tau_{f(u)-u}$  and the $\tau_{i}$  are hyperplane reflections, with $k \ge$ 2n - $3$, and we get a total of 2n - $1$ hyperplane reflections. This proves the induction step. > **Theorem 29.41.** (Cartan–Dieudonn´e, strong form) Let $\phi$  be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on a $K$-vector space $E$ of dimension $n$ (char(K) $\neq  2)$. Then, every isometry $f \in O(\phi$ ) with $f \neq$  id is the composition of at most $n$ hyperplane reflections. To fill in the gap, we need two propositions.

> **Proposition 29.42.** Let $(E, \phi$ ) be an Artinian space of dimension 2m, and let $U$ be a totally isotropic subspace of dimension $m$. For any isometry $f \in O(\phi$ ), if f(U) $= U$, then det(f) $= 1 (f$ is a rotation).

*Proof.* We know that we can find a basis $(u_{1}, \dots  , u_{m}, v_{1}, \dots  , v_{m})$ of $E$ such $(u_{1}, \dots  , u_{m})$ is a basis of $U$ and $\phi$  is represented by the matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_{m} \\ I_{m} & 0 \end{pmatrix} .$$
Since f(U) $= U$, the matrix representing $f$ is of the form
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} B & C \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix} .$$
The condition $A^{\top}A_{m,m}A = A_{m,m}$ translates as
$$\begin{pmatrix} B^{\top} & 0 \\ C^{\top} & D^{\top} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_{m} \\ I_{m} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} B & C \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_{m} \\ I_{m} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
that is,
$$\begin{pmatrix} B^{\top} & 0 \\ C^{\top} & D^{\top} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ D \\ B & C \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & B^{\top}D \\ D^{\top}B & C^{\top}D + D^{\top}C \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_{m} \\ I_{m} & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,$$

which implies that $B^{\top}D =$ I, and so
$$\det(A) = \det(B) \det(D) = \det(B^{\top}) \det(D) = \det(B^{\top}D) = \det(I) = 1,$$
as claimed

> **Proposition 29.43.** Let $\phi$  be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on a space $E$ of dimension $n$, and let $f$ be any isometry $f \in O(\phi$ ) such that f(u)-u is nonzero and isotropic for every nonisotropic vector $u \in E$. Then, $E$ is an Artinian space of dimension $n =$ 2m, and $f$ is a rotation $(f \in SO(\phi$ )).

*Proof.* We follow $E$. Artin’s proof (see $[6]$, Chapter III, Section $4)$. First, consider the case $n = 2$. Since we are assuming that $E$ has some nonzero isotropic vector, by Proposition 29.26, $E$ is an Artinian plane and there is a basis in which $\phi$  is represented by the matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,$$
we have $\phi ((x_{1}, x_{2}), (x_{1}, x_{2})) = 2x_{1}x_{2}$, and the matrices representing isometries are of the form
$$\begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda^{-1} \end{pmatrix} or \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \lambda \\ \lambda^{-1} & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,\lambda  \in K -{0}.$$
In the second case,
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & \lambda \\ \lambda^{-1} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} ,$$
but $u = (\lambda , 1)$ is a nonisotropic vector such that f(u) $-u = 0$. Therefore, we must be in the first case, and det(f) $= +1$. Let us now assume that $n \ge 3$. We are going to prove that f(y) - $y$ is isotropic for all nonzero isotropic vectors $y$. Let $y$ be any nonzero isotropic vector. Since $n \ge 3$, the orthogonal space $(Ky)^{\perp}$ has dimension at least $2$, and we know that rad(Ky) $= rad((Ky)^{\perp})$, a space of dimension at most $1$, which implies that $(Ky)^{\perp}$ contains some nonisotropic vector, say $x$. We have $\phi (x, y) = 0$, so $\phi (x + \epsilon y, x + \epsilon y) = \phi (x$, x)̸ $= 0$, for $\epsilon  = \pm 1$. Then, by hypothesis, the vectors f(x) - $x$, f(x $+ y)$ - $(x + y) =$ f(x) - $x +$ (f(y) - $y)$, and f(x $-y)$ -(x $-y) =$ f(x) $-x$ -(f(y) $-y)$ are isotropic. The last two vectors can be written as f(x) - $x) + \epsilon$ (f(y) - $y)$ with $\epsilon  = \pm 1$, so we have $0 = \phi$ (f(x) - $x) + \epsilon$ (f(y) - $y)$, f(x) - $x) + \epsilon$ (f(y) - $y))$
$$= 2\epsilon \phi (f(x) -x, f(y) -y)) + \epsilon^{2} \phi (f(y) -y, f(y) -y).$$
If we write the two equations corresponding to $\epsilon  = \pm 1$, and then add them up, we get
$$\phi (f(y) -y, f(y) -y) = 0.$$

This proves that f(y)-y is isotropic for any nonzero isotropic vector $y$. Since by hypothesis f(u) $-u$ is isotropic for every nonisotropic vector $u$, we proved that f(u) $-u$ is isotropic for every $u \in E$. If we let $W =$ Im(f - id), then every vector in $W$ is isotropic, and thus $W$ is totally isotropic (recall that we assumed that char(K) $\neq  2$, so $\phi$  is determined by $\Phi$ ). For any $u \in E$ and any $v \in W^{\perp}$ , since $W$ is totally isotropic, we have
$$\phi (f(u) -u, f(v) -v) = 0,$$
and since f(u) - $u \in W$ and $v \in W^{\perp}$ , we have $\phi$ (f(u) - $u, v) = 0$, and so
$$0 = \phi (f(u) -u, f(v) -v)$$
$$= \phi (f(u), f(v)) -\phi (u, f(v)) -\phi (f(u) -u, v)$$
$$= \phi (u, v) -\phi (u, f(v))$$
$$= \phi (u, v -f(v)),$$
for all $u \in E$. Since $\phi$  is nonsingular, this means that f(v) $= v$, for all $v \in W^{\perp}$ . However, by hypothesis, no nonisotropic vector is left fixed, which implies that $W^{\perp}$ is also totally isotropic. In summary, we proved that $W \subseteq W^{\perp}$ and $W^{\perp} \subseteq W^{\perp \perp} = W$, that is,
$$W = W^{\perp} .$$
Since, dim(W) $+ dim(W^{\perp}$ ) $= n$, we conclude that $W$ is a totally isotropic subspace of $E$ such that
$$\dim(W) = n/2.$$
By Proposition 29.29, the space $E$ is an Artinian space of dimension $n =$ 2m. Since $W = W^{\perp}$ and f(W) $= W$, by Proposition 29.42, the isometry $f$ is a rotation.
Remarks: $1$. Another way to finish the proof of Proposition 29.43 is to prove that if $f$ is an isometry, then
$$\mathrm{Ker} (f -id) = (\mathrm{Im}(f -id))^{\perp}.$$
After having proved that $W =$ Im(f - id) is totally isotropic, we get
$$\mathrm{Ker} (f -id) = \mathrm{Im}(f -id),$$
which implies that $(f$ - $id)^{2} = 0$. From this, we deduce that det(f) $= 1$. For details, see Jacobson $[101]$ (Chapter $6$, Section $6). 2$. If $f = \tau_{Hk}  \circ \cdots  \circ \tau_{H1}$ , where the $H_{i}$ are hyperplanes, then it can be shown that
$$\dim(H_{1} \cap H_{2} \cap \cdots  \cap H_{s}) \ge n -s.$$
Now, since each $H_{i}$ is left fixed by $\tau_{Hi}$ , we see that every vector in $H_{1} \cap \cdots  \cap H_{s}$ is left fixed by $f$. In particular, if $s < n$, then $f$ has some nonzero fixed point. As a consequence, an isometry without fixed points requires $n$ hyperplane reflections.
